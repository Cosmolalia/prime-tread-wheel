The Platonic Map

Reading the map
A gold column: no deciding ring marks it. That's a prime. True primes also get a gold tick under the numbers, in every mode. Dim cells: rings that don't decide those numbers yet. A ring starts deciding at the layer that reaches its square. The cream line is that square-root edge. The strip under the numbers is the tread wheel's layers: layer m runs from T(m−1)+1 to T(m). With free phases, a cream column is clean under the current turning. It's no longer a prime.

Shading inside a row is where each number sits on that ring: darkest just past the seam, brightening toward the next one.

What the map shows

    How the margin was measured. Free phases keep ring 2 on the evens, since no even past 2 is prime, and let every odd ring turn. The most the odd rings 3 to p can cover is j − 1 odds in a row, where j is the Jacobsthal function of 3·5·…·p. That's exactly half the published value for 2·3·5·…·p (OEIS A048670). An exact search here agrees with the published values for rings up to 29, and with the halving for rings up to 23. The first wiping stretches come from a full scan from 0 for rings up to 29 and a scan to 3.7 billion for rings up to 31. The settings that wipe out layers past 51 were built by a greedy search and checked number by number.

    The open question. Does every layer hold a prime? That's the conjecture that a prime lies between any two consecutive triangular numbers (OEIS A066888). No layer from 2 to 10,000 is empty.
