Living Prime Map

Jump to any layer of the tread wheel and draw it from scratch. Each prime in the inner half of the wheel drops onto the new layer at one spot (−T(m−1) mod p) and stamps every p-th tick from there. Whatever no ring lands on is prime. Nothing inside the layer is ever divided. In Roll mode the rings carry their landing spots forward layer to layer by adding and wrapping, so the big numbers are never even formed.

Jump works out each ring's landing spot from the layer's starting number, so it stops at layer about 134 million (numbers up to 9·10¹⁵, where browser arithmetic stays exact).

Roll never forms the numbers. Each ring keeps two pointers, where it lands and how far it slides next, and both step by one add-and-wrap per layer. The pointers are dropped in once, where you start; after that it's pure geometry. Its wall is the ring list instead: every prime up to about m/√2 has to be held. Here that's capped at rings up to 200 million (11 million rings), which is layer about 282 million.

The wall's true name. Holding every prime up to √N is not an engineering limit of this engine — it is the price of an exact certificate. Any deterministic method that proves N prime must exclude every prime up to √N; that is the information the certificate contains. The doors past it are not optimizations: probabilistic certificates (Miller–Rabin — no ring list at all, a tiny chance of lying) and structure (decoding the stagger field into something compressed — the standing open problem). Different mechanisms, not a bigger browser tab.

Where the real speedup lives. Skipping layers can't help: a ring's two pointers must each step once per layer no matter what, and locating a ring's next hit on a far window is a quadratic congruence that costs more than the layers it saves. But every ring's state machine is independent, and every register stays under 32 bits — one ring per GPU lane, two uint32s of state each, layers as sequential ticks. That is where Roll stops being a browser trick and becomes the stagger field's native hardware. (Stated as a direction, not built.)

It factors everything it draws: the rings that land on a tick are its prime factors. It can't factor one giant number on its own, because that would need every prime up to the number's square root as a ring.
